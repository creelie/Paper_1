\begin{abstract}
We disprove the conjecture of Bre\v{s}ar, Bujt\'as, Gologranc, Klav\v{z}ar, Ko\v{s}mrlj, Patk\'os, Tuza and Vizer that the Grundy domination number of a strong product of two graphs equals the product of the Grundy domination numbers of the factors. The ratio of the two sides is unbounded, and the smallest counterexample we give is the strong product of the octahedron with a graph on eleven vertices. In the other direction, the Grundy domination number of a strong product is at most the product of the minimum ranks of the factors over any field, so the conjecture holds whenever both factors attain their minimum rank over a common field.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

\enlargethispage{2pt}
Let $G$ be a finite simple graph and write $N[v]$ for the closed neighbourhood of a vertex $v$, the set consisting of $v$ and its neighbours. A sequence $(v_1,\dots,v_k)$ of vertices of $G$ is \emph{legal} if every $v_i$ dominates a vertex that none of its predecessors dominates, that is,
\[
  N[v_i]\setminus\bigcup_{j<i}N[v_j]\neq\emptyset\qquad(1\le i\le k);
\]
a vertex in this difference is a \emph{footprint} of $v_i$.\footnote{A vertex may have several footprints; any one of them witnesses that the step is legal. The vertices of a legal sequence are distinct, since a repeated vertex dominates nothing new.} The \emph{Grundy domination number} $\ggr(G)$ is the length of a longest legal sequence; a longest legal sequence always dominates $G$, since otherwise an undominated vertex could be appended. The parameter was introduced by Bre\v{s}ar, Gologranc, Milani\v{c}, Rall and Rizzi~\cite{BGMRR}, who connected it with the domination game.

In the strong product $G\sprod H$ two distinct vertices $(g,h)$ and $(g',h')$ are adjacent when $g'\in N_G[g]$ and $h'\in N_H[h]$; equivalently $N_{G\sprod H}[(g,h)]=N_G[g]\times N_H[h]$. Taking a Grundy dominating sequence of $G$ and one of $H$ and listing their pairs in lexicographic order gives a legal sequence of $G\sprod H$, so
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower}
  \ggr(G\sprod H)\ \ge\ \ggr(G)\,\ggr(H)
\end{equation}
for all $G$ and $H$~\cite[Proposition~21]{BBG}.\footnote{If $d_i$ footprints $u_i$ in $G$ and $d'_j$ footprints $u'_j$ in $H$, then $(d_i,d'_j)$ footprints $(u_i,u'_j)$ in the lexicographic order of the pairs, because an earlier pair $(d_a,d'_b)$ has $a<i$, so $u_i\notin N[d_a]$, or $a=i$ and $b<j$, so $u'_j\notin N[d'_b]$.} In the same paper the authors conjectured that the bound is always attained.

\begin{conjecture}[{\cite[Conjecture~22]{BBG}}]\label{conj}
For all graphs $G$ and $H$, $\ggr(G\sprod H)=\ggr(G)\,\ggr(H)$.
\end{conjecture}

The conjecture holds when $G$ is a caterpillar~\cite{BBG} and, more generally, when $G$ is a forest, by the work of Bell, Driscoll, Krop and Wolff~\cite{BDKW}. A proof for all graphs was announced by Herrman and Smith~\cite{HS} and later withdrawn.\footnote{The second version of arXiv:2212.04565 (12 January 2023) carries the comment ``There is an error. Some cases were not considered in the proof of Theorem~1.''} We show that the conjecture is false and that no constant multiple of its proposed value is an upper bound.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:unbounded}
For every $C>0$ there are nonempty finite graphs $G,H$ such that
\[
 \ggr(G\sprod H)>C\,\ggr(G)\ggr(H).
\]
\end{theorem}

Section~\ref{sec:unbounded} proves this with two graphs on the five-element subsets of $\{1,\dots,m\}$. Each factor is bounded through an incidence matrix over a finite field whose nonzero entries are exactly the closed neighbourhoods, and the diagonal of the product is an independent set. The ratio grows like $m^2$.

The smallest counterexamples we know have the octahedron $\oct$ as first factor, the complete tripartite graph with three parts of size two. For $N\ge1$ and $r\in\{1,2\}$ let $H^r_N$ be the graph on $\{0,1,\dots,N-1\}$ in which $u$ and $v$ are adjacent when $|u-v|\equiv r\pmod 3$. We compute the Grundy domination numbers of both families for every order.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:family}
For every $N\ge4$ and every $N\ge6$ respectively,
\[
 \ggr(H^1_N)=\Big\lfloor\frac{N}{3}\Big\rfloor+2,\qquad
 \ggr(H^2_N)=\Big\lfloor\frac{N+1}{3}\Big\rfloor+3.
\]
Moreover $\ggr(\oct\sprod H^r_N)\ge N$ for every $N\ge1$ and $r\in\{1,2\}$. Thus Conjecture~\ref{conj} fails for $\oct\sprod H^1_N$ when $N=11$, $13$, $14$ or $N\ge15$, and for $\oct\sprod H^2_N$ when $N=19$ or $N\ge21$.
\end{theorem}

The smallest of these counterexamples is $\oct\sprod H^1_{11}$, with $66$ vertices: the factors have Grundy domination numbers $2$ and $5$, while the product has a legal sequence of length $11$. The proof is in Sections~\ref{sec:graphs} to~\ref{sec:family}. After the first vertex of a legal sequence is chosen in a suitable circulant graph containing $H^r_N$, the undominated vertices form a half graph, and a count at the first selected edge, or an injection at the first selected vertex of the third residue class, gives the upper bound. Explicit sequences attain it. By Proposition~25 of~\cite{BBG}, $\ggr(G\sprod H)\le|V(G)|\,\ggr(H)$, so with the octahedron as first factor the ratio is at most $3$; Theorem~\ref{thm:unbounded} must vary both factors.

Both theorems rest on the same comparison, between legal sequences and matrices. For a field $\F$ and a graph $G$ let $\operatorname{mr}_\F(G)$ be the least rank of a square matrix $M$ over $\F$, with rows and columns indexed by $V(G)$, such that $M_{uv}\ne0$ exactly when $v\in N_G[u]$. Lin showed, through zero forcing, that $\ggr(G)$ is at most the least rank of a real symmetric matrix of this shape~\cite[Theorem~3.1]{Lin19}. The bound passes to strong products.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:rank}
For every field $\F$ and all graphs $G$ and $H$,
\[
 \ggr(G\sprod H)\le\operatorname{mr}_\F(G)\,\operatorname{mr}_\F(H).
\]
In particular, if $\ggr(G)=\operatorname{mr}_\F(G)$ and $\ggr(H)=\operatorname{mr}_\F(H)$ for one field $\F$, then $\ggr(G\sprod H)=\ggr(G)\ggr(H)$.
\end{theorem}

So a counterexample to Conjecture~\ref{conj} needs, for every field, a factor whose Grundy domination number is smaller than its minimum rank over that field. In Theorem~\ref{thm:unbounded} the two factors are bounded over different fields, $\F_2$ and $\F_3$, and this is forced: over a common field the product of the two bounds would also bound the product graph.

The arguments are elementary: rank bounds for matrices with prescribed support, and counting in a half graph. Counts of this kind, attached to a finite structure or a group action, also appear in the author's earlier work~\cite{Bha22a,Bha22b,BB26}.

\section{Rank bounds}\label{sec:rank}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:rank}
Let $G$ be a finite graph and let $M$ be a matrix over a field $\F$, with rows and columns indexed by $V(G)$. Suppose that
\[
 M_{uv}\ne0\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v\in N_G[u].
\]
Then $\ggr(G)\le\operatorname{rank}_{\F}M$. Hence $\ggr(G)\le\operatorname{mr}_\F(G)$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $(v_1,\dots,v_k)$ be legal and choose a footprint $w_i$ for each $v_i$. The footprints are distinct, since $w_j$ is not dominated by $v_1,\dots,v_{j-1}$ while $w_i\in N[v_i]$ for $i<j$. If $i<j$, then $w_j\notin N_G[v_i]$, so $M_{v_iw_j}=0$, and each $M_{v_iw_i}$ is nonzero. The submatrix with rows $v_1,\dots,v_k$ and columns $w_1,\dots,w_k$, in this order, is lower triangular with nonzero diagonal, so its rank is $k$, and $k\le\operatorname{rank}M$.
\end{proof}

For real symmetric matrices this is the inequality of Lin cited above; the direct argument needs neither symmetry nor a particular field. The condition on $M$ cannot be weakened to vanishing on non-edges: the footprint $w_i$ need not equal $v_i$, so the entries $M_{uv}$ with $v$ a neighbour of $u$ must be nonzero as well.\footnote{For an independent set the diagonal alone would do; this is the difference between bounding the independence number, as in the Shannon capacity arguments of Alon~\cite{Alon}, and bounding the Grundy domination number.}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:rank}]
Let $M$ and $M'$ be matrices over $\F$ of ranks $\operatorname{mr}_\F(G)$ and $\operatorname{mr}_\F(H)$ with the supports of Lemma~\ref{lem:rank}. The Kronecker product $M\otimes M'$ has rows and columns indexed by $V(G)\times V(H)$ and entries $M_{gg'}M'_{hh'}$. A product of two elements of a field is nonzero exactly when both are, so this entry is nonzero exactly when $g'\in N_G[g]$ and $h'\in N_H[h]$, that is, when $(g',h')\in N_{G\sprod H}[(g,h)]$. Lemma~\ref{lem:rank} and $\operatorname{rank}(M\otimes M')=\operatorname{rank}M\cdot\operatorname{rank}M'$ give the bound. If both factors attain their minimum ranks, it meets the lower bound~\eqref{eq:lower}.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:tight}
Complete graphs, edgeless graphs and complete bipartite graphs attain their minimum ranks over $\mathbb{C}$, so Conjecture~\ref{conj} holds for every strong product of two such graphs. For $K_n$ the all-ones matrix has rank $1=\ggr(K_n)$, and for the edgeless graph the identity matrix has rank $|V|=\ggr$. For $K_{a,b}$ with $a\ge b\ge1$, the larger side is independent, so listing it gives a legal sequence and $\ggr(K_{a,b})\ge a$. With $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/a}$, the block matrix
\[
 M=\begin{pmatrix} aI_a & Z\\ Z^\ast & I_b\end{pmatrix},\qquad Z_{kj}=\zeta^{kj}\quad(0\le k<a,\ 0\le j<b),
\]
has the support of Lemma~\ref{lem:rank}. Since $Z^\ast Z=aI_b$, its last $b$ rows are $\frac1a Z^\ast$ times its first $a$ rows, so $\operatorname{rank}M=a$ and $\ggr(K_{a,b})=a=\operatorname{mr}_{\mathbb C}(K_{a,b})$.
\end{example}

\section{Unbounded product ratios}\label{sec:unbounded}

To obtain unbounded ratios we vary both factors. The factor bounds come from incidence matrices over different fields, in the manner of the intersection-congruence arguments used by Alon~\cite[Section~3]{Alon} for the Shannon capacity; the application here needs the nonzero entries to be exactly the closed neighbourhoods, so that Lemma~\ref{lem:rank} applies. No result about the Shannon capacity is used.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:two-fields}
For $m\ge5$, let $A_m$ and $B_m$ have vertex set $\binom{[m]}5$, the five-element subsets of $[m]=\{1,\dots,m\}$. Distinct sets $S,T$ are adjacent in $A_m$ when $|S\cap T|$ is odd, and in $B_m$ when $|S\cap T|\equiv2\pmod3$. Then
\[
 \ggr(A_m)\le m,\qquad \ggr(B_m)\le\binom m2,\qquad
 \ggr(A_m\sprod B_m)\ge\binom m5.
\]
In particular,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:unbounded-ratio}
 \frac{\ggr(A_m\sprod B_m)}{\ggr(A_m)\ggr(B_m)}
 \ge\frac{(m-2)(m-3)(m-4)}{60m},
\end{equation}
so the ratios are unbounded.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Let $U$ be the incidence matrix between five-element subsets of $[m]$ and elements of $[m]$, with $U_{Sx}=1$ when $x\in S$. Over $\F_2$,
\[
 (UU^{\mathsf T})_{ST}=|S\cap T|\pmod2.
\]
Since $|S|=5$ is odd, the diagonal entries are nonzero, and an off-diagonal entry is nonzero exactly when $|S\cap T|$ is odd, that is, when $S$ and $T$ are adjacent in $A_m$. So the nonzero entries of $UU^{\mathsf T}$ are exactly the closed neighbourhoods of $A_m$. Its rank is at most the number $m$ of columns of $U$, and Lemma~\ref{lem:rank} gives the first bound.

Let $V$ be the incidence matrix between five-element subsets and two-element subsets of $[m]$, with $V_{SP}=1$ when $P\subseteq S$. Over $\F_3$,
\[
 (VV^{\mathsf T})_{ST}=\binom{|S\cap T|}{2}\pmod3,
\]
the number of two-element subsets of $S\cap T$. For intersection sizes $t=0,1,2,3,4,5$ the values $\binom t2=0,0,1,3,6,10$ reduce to $0,0,1,0,0,1$ modulo $3$. So the entry is nonzero exactly when $S=T$ or $|S\cap T|=2$, which are precisely the closed neighbourhoods of $B_m$. The rank is at most $\binom m2$, proving the second bound.

The product vertices
\[
 \mathcal D_m=\{(S,S):S\in\tbinom{[m]}5\}
\]
form an independent set of $A_m\sprod B_m$: for $S\ne T$, adjacency of $(S,S)$ and $(T,T)$ would need $S$ and $T$ adjacent in both factors, which would require $|S\cap T|$ to be $1$ or $3$ and also $2$. Listing the vertices of $\mathcal D_m$ in any order gives a legal sequence, each vertex footprinting itself, because no earlier vertex of the list dominates it. This proves the lower bound $\binom m5$ for the product.

The three bounds give~\eqref{eq:unbounded-ratio}, since
\begin{align*}
&\binom m5\Big/\Bigl(m\binom m2\Bigr)\\
 &=\frac{m(m-1)(m-2)(m-3)(m-4)}{120}\cdot\frac{2}{m^2(m-1)}\\
 &=\frac{(m-2)(m-3)(m-4)}{60m},
\end{align*}
and the right side tends to infinity with $m$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:unbounded}]
Take $G=A_m$ and $H=B_m$ with $m$ so large that $(m-2)(m-3)(m-4)>60Cm$.
\end{proof}

For example, $m=13$ gives $\ggr(A_{13}\sprod B_{13})\ge1287$, whereas $\ggr(A_{13})\ggr(B_{13})\le13\cdot78=1014$. The two factor bounds use different fields; no matrix product over a common field is involved, and nothing is claimed about the exact values of $\ggr(A_m)$ and $\ggr(B_m)$.


\section{The octahedron and the graphs \texorpdfstring{$H^r_N$}{HrN}}\label{sec:graphs}

We write the vertices of the octahedron as $g_0,g_1,g_2,x_0,x_1,x_2$, where $x_t$ is the antipode of $g_t$; the three antipodal pairs are the only non-adjacent pairs (Figure~\ref{fig:oct}). Thus $N[v]$ is everything except the antipode of $v$.

\begin{figure}[ht]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{figures/octahedron.pdf}
\caption{The octahedron $\oct$. Each vertex is adjacent to all others except its antipode; the dotted segments join the antipodal pairs $\{g_t,x_t\}$.}\label{fig:oct}
\end{figure}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:oct}
$\ggr(\oct)=2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The sequence $(g_0,x_0)$ is legal: $x_0$ footprints itself. Conversely, let $(u,v)$ be legal. If $v$ is the antipode of $u$, then $N[u]\cup N[v]$ is the whole vertex set; otherwise $N[u]$ misses only the antipode of $u$, which lies in $N[v]$. Either way nothing is left for a third vertex to footprint.
\end{proof}

In $H^r_N$ we write $a_i=3i$, $b_i=3i+1$ and $c_i=3i+2$. Two vertices of the same residue class modulo $3$ are never adjacent. For vertices of different classes, a difference $3k+e$ with $k<0$ and $e\in\{1,2\}$ has absolute value $3|k|-e\equiv3-e\pmod3$, and this gives
\begin{align}
 \text{in }H^1_N:&\quad a_i\sim b_j\iff j\ge i,\qquad b_i\sim c_j\iff j\ge i,\qquad c_i\sim a_j\iff j>i,\label{eq:adj1}\\
 \text{in }H^2_N:&\quad a_i\sim c_j\iff j\ge i,\qquad b_i\sim a_j\iff j>i,\qquad c_i\sim b_j\iff j>i,\label{eq:adj2}
\end{align}
from $b_j-a_i=c_j-b_i=3(j-i)+1$ and $a_j-c_i=3(j-i-1)+1$ in the first line, and $c_j-a_i=3(j-i)+2$ and $a_j-b_i=b_j-c_i=3(j-i-1)+2$ in the second.

\section{A long legal sequence in the product}\label{sec:product}

The construction uses one property of $H^r_N$. Colour $v$ by $t(v)=-v\bmod3$ in $H^1_N$ and by $t(v)=v\bmod3$ in $H^2_N$. If $uv$ is an edge with $u<v$, then $v-u\equiv r\pmod3$, and in both cases $t(u)\equiv t(v)+1\pmod3$. We state the construction for any graph with this property.

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:product}
Let $H$ be a graph with vertices $v_0,\dots,v_{N-1}$ and a map $t\colon V(H)\to\Z_3$ such that $t(v_i)\equiv t(v_j)+1\pmod3$ for every edge $v_iv_j$ with $i<j$. Then the sequence
\[
  s_j=\big(g_{t(v_j)},\,v_j\big),\qquad j=0,1,\dots,N-1,
\]
is legal in $\oct\sprod H$, and $\ggr(\oct\sprod H)\ge N$. In particular $\ggr(\oct\sprod H^r_N)\ge N$ for every $N\ge1$ and $r\in\{1,2\}$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
We show that $s_j$ footprints $f_j=(x_{t(v_j)+1},v_j)$, the indices of $g$ and $x$ being taken modulo $3$. First, $f_j\in N[s_j]$: the vertex $x_{t(v_j)+1}$ is not the antipode $x_{t(v_j)}$ of $g_{t(v_j)}$, so it lies in $N[g_{t(v_j)}]$, and $v_j\in N[v_j]$. Now let $i<j$. If $f_j\in N[s_i]$, then $x_{t(v_j)+1}\in N[g_{t(v_i)}]$, which means $t(v_i)\ne t(v_j)+1$, and $v_j\in N[v_i]$, which for $i\ne j$ means that $v_iv_j$ is an edge, hence $t(v_i)=t(v_j)+1$. The two conclusions contradict each other, so $f_j\notin N[s_i]$.
\end{proof}

Figure~\ref{fig:product} shows the sequence for $H^2_{21}$. Every column of the product contains exactly one vertex of the sequence, while a sequence of the form used for~\eqref{eq:lower} uses only $\ggr(H^2_{21})=10$ columns. The rotation of the three colours along the sequence lets each new vertex reach a footprint in a layer $x_t$ that none of the earlier vertices covers in that column.

\section{The Grundy domination numbers of \texorpdfstring{$H^1_N$}{H1N} and \texorpdfstring{$H^2_N$}{H2N}}\label{sec:family}

A legal sequence in an induced subgraph remains legal in the larger graph, with the same footprints, since closed neighbourhoods in the subgraph are the intersections of those in the larger graph with the subgraph. We use this observation for both bounds. The upper bounds come from one lemma about half graphs.

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:half}
Let $q\ge1$, $\varepsilon\in\{0,1\}$, and let $z$ be a vertex of a graph $\Gamma$ whose non-neighbours are $2q$ vertices $X_i,Y_j$ ($0\le i,j<q$). Suppose that there are no edges among the $X_i$ and none among the $Y_j$, that
\begin{equation}\label{eq:half}
 X_i\sim Y_j\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad i\ge j,
\end{equation}
and that every neighbour $w$ of $z$ has an index $k\in\{0,\dots,q\}$ with
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wk}
 N[w]\cap\{X_i,Y_j\}=\{X_i:i<k\}\cup\{Y_j:k+\varepsilon\le j<q\}.
\end{equation}
Then every legal sequence of $\Gamma$ that begins with $z$ has length at most $q+2+\varepsilon$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
The vertex $z$ dominates every vertex except the $X_i$ and $Y_j$, and the $2q$ vertices $X_i,Y_j$ induce a half graph with the perfect matching $X_iY_i$ (Figure~\ref{fig:half}). Follow the sequence from its second vertex on until either a neighbour of $z$ is selected or the selected $X$- and $Y$-vertices contain an edge for the first time. Before either event, let $I$ and $J$ be the sets of selected indices on the two sides, and put $r=|I|$, $s=|J|$. By~\eqref{eq:half}, the absence of edges means
\begin{equation}\label{eq:independent}
 i<j\qquad(i\in I,\ j\in J).
\end{equation}
Each legal step dominates at least one vertex that was undominated, so the number of steps after a given moment is at most the number of vertices undominated at that moment. We treat the possible events in turn.

\emph{A new $X_i$ creates an edge.} Then $J\ne\emptyset$ and $b=\min J\le i$. All earlier $X$-indices are less than $b$ by~\eqref{eq:independent}, so $i$ is the largest selected $X$-index. After this step, an $X$-vertex is dominated if it was selected or if it is adjacent to a selected $Y_j$, that is, if its index is at least $b$; so the undominated $X$-indices are $\{0,\dots,b-1\}\setminus I$. A $Y$-vertex is dominated if it was selected or is adjacent to a selected $X$-vertex, and the selected $X$-vertex with the largest index is $X_i$; so the undominated $Y$-indices are those greater than $i$ that are not in $J$. Writing $s_0=|J\cap\{b,\dots,i\}|$ for the number of selected $Y$-indices at most $i$, the number of undominated vertices is
\[
 (b-r)+(q-1-i)-(s-s_0).
\]
The number of vertices selected after $z$, which is $r+s+1$, plus the number still undominated, is
\[
r+s+1+b-r+q-1-i-s+s_0=q+b-i+s_0\le q+1,
\]
because $s_0\le i-b+1$. The full sequence, including $z$, has length at most $q+2$.

\emph{A new $Y_j$ creates an edge.} The map $X_i\mapsto Y_{q-1-i}$, $Y_j\mapsto X_{q-1-j}$ exchanges the two sides and preserves~\eqref{eq:half}, since $q-1-j\ge q-1-i$ exactly when $i\ge j$. Up to this step only $X$- and $Y$-vertices were selected after $z$, so the count of the previous case applies, and again the length is at most $q+2$.

\emph{A neighbour $w$ of $z$ is selected first.} Let $k$ be its index in~\eqref{eq:wk}. After this step every vertex still undominated belongs to
\[
W_k=\{X_i:k\le i<q\}\cup\{Y_j:0\le j<\min(k+\varepsilon,q)\},\qquad |W_k|\le q+\varepsilon.
\]
At least $r+s$ vertices of $W_k$ were dominated already before $w$ was selected. To see this, map each earlier selection by
\[
 X_i\longmapsto\begin{cases}X_i,&i\ge k,\\Y_i,&i<k,\end{cases}
 \qquad
 Y_j\longmapsto\begin{cases}Y_j,&j<k+\varepsilon,\\X_j,&j\ge k+\varepsilon.\end{cases}
\]
Each image lies in $W_k$ and is either the selected vertex itself or its partner in the matching, which the selected vertex dominates by~\eqref{eq:half}. Images of distinct selections on the same side are distinct. An image shared by a selection $X_i$ and a selection $Y_j$ would force $i=j$, with $i\in I$ and $j\in J$, contrary to~\eqref{eq:independent}. So the map is injective, at most $q+\varepsilon-r-s$ vertices remain undominated after $w$, and the sequence has length at most
\[
 1+(r+s)+1+(q+\varepsilon-r-s)=q+2+\varepsilon.
\]

\emph{Neither event occurs.} Then the vertices selected after $z$ form an independent set of the half graph, which has the perfect matching $X_iY_i$, so there are at most $q$ of them and the sequence has length at most $q+1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:upper}
For every integer $q\ge1$, $\ggr(H^1_{3q+2})\le q+2$ and $\ggr(H^2_{3q+1})\le q+3$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Let $r\in\{1,2\}$ and $n=3q+3-r$, that is, $n=3q+2$ for $r=1$ and $n=3q+1$ for $r=2$. In both cases $n\equiv 2r\pmod3$, so for $1\le d<n$ the conditions $d\equiv r$ and $n-d\equiv r\pmod3$ are equivalent. Hence $u$ and $v$ are adjacent in $H^r_n$ exactly when $(u-v)\bmod n$ is congruent to $r$ modulo $3$, and translation modulo $n$ preserves adjacency. A legal sequence stays legal under a graph automorphism, so we may translate a nonempty legal sequence so that its first vertex is $z=0$. In both graphs put
\[
 X_i=a_{i+1}=3(i+1),\qquad 0\le i<q.
\]

In $H^1_{3q+2}$ the neighbours of $0$ are the $b_k=3k+1$, $0\le k\le q$, and its non-neighbours are the $X_i$ and the vertices $Y_j=c_j=3j+2$, $0\le j<q$. By~\eqref{eq:adj1}, $X_i=a_{i+1}\sim c_j$ exactly when $i+1>j$, which is~\eqref{eq:half}; and $b_k$ is adjacent to $a_{i+1}$ exactly when $i+1\le k$ and to $c_j$ exactly when $j\ge k$, which is~\eqref{eq:wk} with $\varepsilon=0$. Lemma~\ref{lem:half} gives $q+2$.

In $H^2_{3q+1}$ the neighbours of $0$ are the $c_k=3k+2$, $0\le k<q$, and its non-neighbours are the $X_i$ and the vertices $Y_j=b_j=3j+1$, $0\le j<q$. By~\eqref{eq:adj2}, $a_{i+1}\sim b_j$ exactly when $i+1>j$, which is~\eqref{eq:half}; and $c_k$ is adjacent to $a_{i+1}$ exactly when $i+1\le k$ and to $b_j$ exactly when $j>k$, which is~\eqref{eq:wk} with $\varepsilon=1$. Lemma~\ref{lem:half} gives $q+3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:lower1}
If $N\ge4$ and $q=\lfloor N/3\rfloor$, then $\ggr(H^1_N)\ge q+2$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $q=1$, the sequence $(0,1,2)$ in $H^1_4$, a path, has the footprints $0,2,3$. For $q=2$, the sequence $(0,5,1,2)$ in $H^1_6$ has the footprints $0,5,2,3$. For $q\ge3$ we work in $H^1_{3q}$, an induced subgraph of $H^1_N$, whose vertices are $a_i,b_i,c_i$ for $0\le i<q$, and take
\[
 a_0,\ a_1,\ c_0,\ c_1,\ c_2,\dots,c_{q-2},\ b_0,
\]
of length $q+2$. By~\eqref{eq:adj1}, $a_0$ dominates itself and every $b_j$, and $a_1$ dominates itself and $c_0$; the vertex $c_0$ then footprints $a_2$, which neither $a_0$ nor $a_1$ dominates. For $1\le m\le q-2$ the vertex $c_m$ is adjacent to no $a_i$ with $i\le1$ and to no other $c$, so it footprints itself. Finally $b_0$ is adjacent to every $c_j$, and $c_{q-1}$ has not been dominated, so it is the footprint of $b_0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}\label{lem:lower2}
If $N\ge6$ and $q=\lfloor(N+1)/3\rfloor$, then $\ggr(H^2_N)\ge q+3$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
For $q=2$, the sequence $(1,3,5,0,2)$ in $H^2_6$ has the footprints $1,5,0,2,4$ and remains legal in $H^2_7$. For $q=3$, the sequence $(0,7,2,4,5,1)$ in $H^2_8$ has the footprints $0,7,4,6,3,1$ and remains legal in $H^2_9$ and $H^2_{10}$. Each footprint can be read off from~\eqref{eq:adj2}.

For $q\ge4$ it suffices to work in $H^2_{3q-1}$, which is an induced subgraph of $H^2_N$ because $3q-1\le N$. Its vertices are $a_i,b_i$ for $0\le i\le q-1$ and $c_i$ for $0\le i\le q-2$. Take the sequence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:lower-sequence}
 a_0,a_1,\dots,a_{q-3},\ b_{q-1},\ c_{q-3},\ c_0,\ a_{q-2},\ b_0 .
\end{equation}
The first $q-2$ vertices footprint themselves, since vertices of one class are never adjacent. The last five have the successive footprints
\[
 b_{q-1},\quad b_{q-2},\quad b_{q-3},\quad a_{q-2},\quad a_{q-1},
\]
by~\eqref{eq:adj2}: $b_{q-1}$ is adjacent to no $a_i$ with $i\le q-3$, so it is undominated and footprints itself; $b_{q-2}$ is adjacent to $c_{q-3}$ and not to any earlier vertex of the sequence; $b_{q-3}$ is adjacent to $c_0$, because $q-3>0$, and not to $a_0,\dots,a_{q-3}$, $b_{q-1}$ or $c_{q-3}$; neither $a_{q-2}$ nor $a_{q-1}$ is adjacent to $b_{q-1}$, $c_{q-3}$ or $c_0$, so $a_{q-2}$ footprints itself and $b_0$ footprints $a_{q-1}$. The sequence has length $(q-2)+5=q+3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:family}]
For $H^1_N$ with $N\ge4$ put $q=\lfloor N/3\rfloor\ge1$. Then $N\le3q+2$, so $H^1_N$ is an induced subgraph of $H^1_{3q+2}$, and Lemmas~\ref{lem:upper} and~\ref{lem:lower1} give $\ggr(H^1_N)=q+2$. For $H^2_N$ with $N\ge6$ put $q=\lfloor(N+1)/3\rfloor\ge2$. Then $N\le3q+1$, and Lemmas~\ref{lem:upper} and~\ref{lem:lower2} give $\ggr(H^2_N)=q+3$. The product bound is Proposition~\ref{prop:product}.

The conjecture fails for $\oct\sprod H^r_N$ whenever $N>2\ggr(H^r_N)$, by Lemma~\ref{lem:oct}. For $r=1$ write $N=3q+e$ with $e\in\{0,1,2\}$; then $N-2(q+2)=q+e-4$ is positive exactly when $q\ge5-e$, that is, for $N=11$, $13$, $14$ and every $N\ge15$. For $r=2$ write $N=3q-1$, $3q$ or $3q+1$; the differences $N-2(q+3)$ are $q-7$, $q-6$ and $q-5$, positive for $N=19$ and every $N\ge21$.
\end{proof}

\begin{example}\label{ex:small}
For $N=11$ the sequence of Lemma~\ref{lem:lower1} with $q=3$ is $(0,3,2,5,1)$, of length $5=\ggr(H^1_{11})$, while $\oct\sprod H^1_{11}$, with $66$ vertices, has the legal sequence $s_j=(g_{(-j)\bmod 3},j)$, $0\le j\le10$, of length $11$; the footprint of $s_j$ is $(x_{(1-j)\bmod3},j)$. For $N=19$ the sequence~\eqref{eq:lower-sequence} with $q=6$ is $(0,3,6,9,16,\allowbreak11,2,12,1)$, of length $9=\ggr(H^2_{19})$, and $\oct\sprod H^2_{19}$ has $114$ vertices and a legal sequence of length $19$. For $N=12$ and $r=1$, and for $N=20$ and $r=2$, the formula gives exactly $N=2\ggr(H^r_N)$, and Theorem~\ref{thm:family} says nothing.
\end{example}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:computed}
The following values were found by exhaustive computer search and are not used in the proofs. For $4\le N\le13$, $\ggr(\oct\sprod H^1_N)$ equals $6,6,8,8,8,10,10,11,12,13$, which is $2\ggr(H^1_N)$ for $N\le10$ and $N=12$; and $\ggr(\oct\sprod H^2_N)=2\ggr(H^2_N)$ for $6\le N\le12$. So the length $N$ of Proposition~\ref{prop:product} is optimal for $\oct\sprod H^1_{11}$ and $\oct\sprod H^1_{13}$.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:scope}
Everything above is proved in full. Theorem~\ref{thm:unbounded} rests on Lemma~\ref{lem:rank} and the two incidence matrices; Theorem~\ref{thm:family} on the half-graph count of Lemma~\ref{lem:half}, the explicit sequences of Lemmas~\ref{lem:lower1} and~\ref{lem:lower2}, and Proposition~\ref{prop:product}. The results quoted from elsewhere are the inequality~\eqref{eq:lower} and the bound $\ggr(G\sprod H)\le|V(G)|\ggr(H)$, both from~\cite{BBG} with proofs of a few lines, and Lin's inequality, which Lemma~\ref{lem:rank} reproves; none of them is needed for the counterexamples. Nothing is claimed about the exact values of $\ggr(A_m)$, $\ggr(B_m)$ or $\ggr(A_m\sprod B_m)$ beyond the three bounds of Theorem~\ref{thm:two-fields}, nor about the smallest possible counterexample.
\end{remark}

\section{Questions}

\begin{question}
Which graphs $G$ satisfy Conjecture~\ref{conj} for every $H$? All forests do~\cite{BDKW}, and by Theorem~\ref{thm:rank} so does every $G$ with $\ggr(G)=\operatorname{mr}_\F(G)$ for every field $\F$, such as complete and edgeless graphs; the octahedron does not.
\end{question}

\begin{question}
Is there a counterexample to Conjecture~\ref{conj} with fewer than $66$ vertices?
\end{question}
